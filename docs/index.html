Reading dopamine transporter scans with machines

Our entry to the DrivenData DaT Parkinson's Challenge: a registration-based imaging pipeline, a small ensemble of 2D networks, and about sixty experiments — most of which didn't work.

The task

A DaT SPECT scan shows where a radioactive tracer binds to dopamine transporters. In a healthy brain it collects in the striatum as two bright, comma-shaped structures. In Parkinsonian syndromes the tail of that comma (the putamen) fades first.

The challenge supplied 1,362 labelled scans from ten French hospitals, each read by experts as normal or abnormal. Submissions were code that ran offline on hidden test scans and returned a probability for each; the score was log loss. Scans came from many cameras, with voxel sizes from 1.4 mm to 4.4 mm and more than 90 combinations of matrix size and spacing.

Results

We finished 10th on the private leaderboard, up from 15th on the public one, with a best private log loss of 0.2760. Every submission scored worse on the public test scans than in local cross-validation, and about 0.04 worse again on the private scans; other teams saw the same. The order of our submissions barely changed from one board to the other, so the improvements were real: 0.2851 to 0.2760 on the private board from the first submission to the last.

The final pipeline

One shared library does the preprocessing for both training and inference, so the two can't drift apart. Every test scan is processed on its own, as the rules require.

  1. Load and orientRead the NIfTI, reorient to RAS, average any 4-D frames.
  2. Find the striatumHead mask, vertex, then the brightest smoothed voxel in a band below it. The midline comes from the head, not the uptake, so one-sided loss can't pull it off-centre.
  3. Normalise and cropDivide by a trimmed background mean; resample once to a 2 mm, 112×160×160 mm box.
  4. RegisterNine-parameter affine alignment on the GPU to a symmetric template built from normal training scans.
  5. MeasureCaudate and anterior/posterior putamen binding ratios, extents and asymmetry: 173 left/right-invariant features.
  6. Predict2D EfficientNets on axial slabs (standard and dual-normalised), a slice-sequence network, LightGBM and a logistic model.
  7. StackA logistic regression on out-of-fold outputs combines the families into a calibrated probability.

The slice-sequence network

Nine 4 mm axial slices, from 16 mm below to 16 mm above the striatal peak, each pass through a shared ImageNet EfficientNet-B0. A bidirectional GRU reads them in order and gated attention decides which slices matter. Each slice carries three channels: background-normalised, self-normalised, and a 12 mm local context.

Why the ensemble looks the way it does

The final model averages 135 CNN checkpoints and 90 tabular models, bagged over two fold splits and 10-fold runs. One explicit weight table produces both the stacker's training inputs and the deployed model, and an assertion checks that the two agree.

What worked, and why

Five changes improved the model by more than run-to-run noise. Each fixed a specific weakness rather than adding capacity.

Template registration and regional binding ratios

0.2319 → 0.2153 in local CV. Aligning every scan to a common template lets us measure the caudate and putamen separately, the same quantities expert readers judge. The single best feature, worst-side posterior putamen uptake, reached an AUROC of 0.92 alone.

Strong "unrealistic" blur and noise augmentation

0.3529 → 0.2716 for CNNs on scanner types they never saw. Following Buddenkotte and Buchert (J Nucl Med, 2024), blurring and adding noise far beyond realistic levels taught the networks to ignore camera resolution. It was the change that moved the leaderboard most.

Dual normalisation

0.2372 → 0.2249 for a single CNN. Extra channels divided by each scan's own 99th percentile give a second view of intensity. A mathematically "cleaner" version that removed all dependence on the background estimate did worse, so the original design stayed.

The slice-sequence network

0.2014 → 0.1988 for the ensemble, and −0.0039 on the public leaderboard. Averaging three thick slabs throws away how uptake changes from bottom to top; reading thin slices in order keeps it. It is also less correlated with the other CNNs (0.94), so it adds new information.

Bagging over fold splits

about −0.002 each time. Training the whole ensemble again on a different partition and averaging reduced variance, with a small but real leaderboard gain.

Lessons

Measure noise before believing a gain

Two identical training runs differed by 0.004 in log loss. Several "improvements" were smaller than that and vanished once three seeds were averaged. Comparisons were made paired, on the same folds and seeds, with a bootstrap of the per-scan differences.

A proxy for the leaderboard helps, until it doesn't

Holding out whole scanner types reproduced one public score almost exactly (0.2444 against 0.2446) and correctly flagged strong augmentation as a big win. It over-predicted later gains, so it guided choices rather than replacing submissions.

Check that what you evaluate is what you ship

An external review found the deployed ensemble weighted two runs 2:1 while validation weighted them 1:1. The effect was negligible (0.2015 against 0.2014), but it led to one weight table that drives both, with an assertion. An evaluation leak in a bagged comparison also once produced a fake 0.15; results that look too good get checked first.

Simulations are not evidence

A label-noise simulation predicted that calibration would gain 0.009 on the leaderboard. The real submission lost 0.0003 on the public board. From then on, a change needed a measured local gain before it earned a submission.

The public board can mislead in both directions

The same calibrated submission turned out better than its uncalibrated twin on the private board (0.2794 against 0.2806). Softer probabilities help when the test scans are harder than the training scans. With a small public set, it is worth keeping one mildly calibrated variant among the final submissions even when the public score doesn't reward it.

Reproduce it

The repository has the full code, the nine training configurations behind the final ensemble, and one script that rebuilds and verifies it.

# 1. put the competition data in data/niftis/, data/train_labels.csv and data/smoke/
# 2. Python 3.12, PyTorch for your CUDA version, then:
pip install -r requirements.txt
# 3. preprocess, train, bag and verify (about 5 hours on one 8 GB GPU)
bash reproduce.sh

Every submission was checked the same way before upload: a clean unzip, a run on the organisers' smoke data, a forced failure of every preprocessing worker, a parity check against the training pipeline, and an import under the competition's Python 3.12 package versions.

Data and rules

The repository and this page contain code and whole-dataset aggregate metrics only. No scans, labels, per-scan predictions, derived features or trained weights are published. The only pretrained weights are ImageNet checkpoints from timm.
